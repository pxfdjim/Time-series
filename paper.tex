\begin{abstract}
Time-series anomaly detection aims to identify abnormal segments that deviate from normal temporal behaviors. Recent multimodal methods have shown the potential of incorporating textual information for anomaly detection. However, external textual sources, such as logs, events, or manual descriptions, are often noisy, weakly aligned with numerical signals, and mixed with task-irrelevant semantics, which may interfere with reconstruction-based anomaly scoring. To address this issue, we propose a text-temporal multimodal reconstruction framework for time-series anomaly detection. Instead of relying on external textual annotations or directly asking language models to judge anomalies, our framework derives textual descriptions from the time series itself to summarize segment-level temporal behaviors. The descriptions are encoded by a frozen large language model and aligned with numerical patch representations through a probabilistic text-temporal alignment objective. The aligned textual features are then incorporated into a cross-modal masked reconstruction module to guide temporal patch recovery. During inference, reconstruction errors are used as anomaly scores. Experiments on benchmark datasets demonstrate that our framework consistently improves anomaly detection performance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Time-series anomaly detection (TSAD) aims to identify observations or segments that deviate from expected temporal behaviors, and has been widely studied in anomaly detection research \cite{chandola2009anomaly,blazquez2021review}. It plays a critical role in real-world monitoring scenarios such as spacecraft telemetry, industrial systems, cloud services, cyber-physical systems, and large-scale sensor networks \cite{hundman2018detecting,zhong2023survey}. Since abnormal events are usually rare, diverse, and expensive to annotate, unsupervised reconstruction-based methods have become a widely adopted paradigm. Representative studies learn normal temporal patterns through density estimation, reconstruction, or hierarchical representation learning, such as DAGMM \cite{zong2018deep}, MSCRED \cite{zhang2019mscred}, and THOC \cite{shen2020time}. These methods show that modeling regular temporal dynamics from historical observations is effective for detecting abnormal deviations.

Despite their progress, most reconstruction-based TSAD methods still operate mainly in the numerical space. They recover raw values or latent representations from corrupted inputs, while the high-level behaviors behind a time-series window are usually learned only implicitly. In practice, anomalies may not only appear as point-wise deviations, but also as abnormal local variation, trend evolution, periodic dependency, lag dependency, or component-level changes. Prior time-series modeling studies have shown that decomposition, periodicity, frequency-domain structures, and temporal dependencies are essential for understanding sequential data \cite{wu2021autoformer,zhou2022fedformer,gao2020robusttad}. However, these temporal structures are rarely transformed into explicit textual representations for reconstruction-based anomaly detection. As a result, existing methods may overlook useful behavioral cues that are difficult to capture from numerical reconstruction alone.

Recent advances in foundation models, language models, and multimodal learning provide new opportunities for introducing textual information into time-series modeling. TimeGPT, MOMENT, and Lag-Llama demonstrate the potential of large-scale pretraining for general time-series analysis \cite{garza2023timegpt,goswami2024moment,rasul2024lagllama}, while recent studies also begin to investigate whether language models can understand time-series anomalies \cite{zhou2024can}. However, existing attempts to incorporate textual information still face several limitations. Directly serializing raw numerical values into text usually produces lengthy inputs with limited behavioral abstraction, while methods relying on external textual information may suffer from noise, redundancy, and weak alignment with the target time-series window. More importantly, textual information is often used as auxiliary prompts or decision-level cues, which is insufficient for reconstruction-based TSAD where anomaly scoring depends on temporal recovery. Therefore, textual information should be derived from the time series itself and transformed into reconstruction-compatible signals that can interact with numerical patch representations.


To bridge the gap between textual temporal information and numerical temporal recovery, we propose a text-temporal reconstruction framework for time-series anomaly detection. Given an input window, we divide the time series into local patches and construct dual-view textual information directly from the time series itself. The behavior description view summarizes segment-level temporal patterns, while the distribution prediction view transforms textual information into reconstruction-oriented predictive distributions. These text-derived distributions are then aligned with numerical patch representations to guide masked temporal reconstruction. In this way, our method converts time-series-derived textual information into reconstruction-compatible signals, allowing textual information to directly contribute to reconstruction-based anomaly scoring.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item We propose a text-temporal reconstruction framework for TSAD that incorporates time-series-derived textual information into masked temporal reconstruction, moving beyond purely numerical patch recovery.


\item We design a dual-view text construction strategy from the input time-series window, where the behavior description view summarizes segment-level temporal patterns and the distribution prediction view converts textual information into reconstruction-oriented predictive distributions.

\item We develop a distribution-guided fusion mechanism that aligns text-derived predictive distributions with numerical patch representations, enabling textual information to guide masked patch recovery and improve reconstruction-based anomaly scoring.


\end{itemize}







\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Time-Series Anomaly Detection}

Time-series anomaly detection (TSAD) aims to identify observations or segments that deviate from normal temporal patterns, and has been widely studied in industrial monitoring, cloud systems, cyber-physical systems, and sensor networks. Deep TSAD methods commonly follow reconstruction-, prediction-, density-estimation-, or representation-learning-based paradigms. OmniAnomaly models multivariate temporal dynamics with stochastic recurrent networks and detects anomalies using reconstruction probability \cite{su2019robust}, while USAD introduces adversarially trained autoencoders for efficient unsupervised anomaly detection \cite{audibert2020usad}. To better capture dependencies among variables, MTAD-GAT combines forecasting and reconstruction with temporal and feature-oriented graph attention \cite{zhao2020mtadgat}, and GDN learns sensor relationships to detect deviations from graph-based normal behavior \cite{deng2021graph}. InterFusion further models inter-metric and temporal dependencies through hierarchical latent variables \cite{li2021interfusion}. More recently, Transformer-based methods have improved long-range dependency modeling. Anomaly Transformer proposes association discrepancy to distinguish abnormal temporal associations \cite{xu2022anomaly}, TranAD adopts self-conditioning and adversarial training for multivariate anomaly detection \cite{tuli2022tranad}, and DCdetector explores dual-attention contrastive representation learning for anomaly discrimination \cite{yang2023dcdetector}.

Another relevant line of work is patch-based and masked time-series modeling. PatchTST represents time series as local patches, reducing attention cost while preserving local temporal semantics \cite{nie2023time}. TimesNet transforms one-dimensional series into two-dimensional temporal variations to capture multi-periodic patterns \cite{wu2023timesnet}. Ti-MAE and SimMTM show that masked reconstruction can encourage models to learn informative temporal representations from corrupted time-series inputs \cite{li2023timae,dong2023simmtm}. These studies suggest that local patches, temporal variations, periodic structures, and masked recovery are important for time-series representation learning. However, most existing TSAD methods still operate mainly in the numerical space. Segment-level behaviors such as local variation, trend evolution, periodic dependency, lag dependency, and component-level changes are usually learned implicitly by neural networks rather than explicitly represented. This motivates our work to construct textual behavioral descriptions from the time series itself and use them to assist reconstruction-based anomaly detection.
\subsection{Multimodal Time-Series Analysis}

Recent studies have explored modeling time series beyond purely numerical representations by introducing language models and multimodal learning. Some methods reformulate time-series forecasting into language-compatible tasks, such as sentence-to-sentence prediction or numerical value serialization \cite{xue2024promptcast,gruver2023large}. Others tokenize time series or adapt pretrained foundation models through alignment and reprogramming, enabling language-model architectures to process temporal data \cite{ansari2024chronos,zhou2023one,sun2024test,jin2024timellm}. In addition, decomposition-aware prompting and temporal structure modeling have been introduced to better adapt language models to time-series analysis \cite{cao2024tempo}. These studies show the potential of language-model priors for temporal modeling, but they mainly focus on forecasting or general representation learning rather than reconstruction-based anomaly detection.

Multimodal time-series analysis further investigates how numerical signals interact with auxiliary modalities such as text, images, events, and domain knowledge \cite{jia2024gpt4mts,zhang2024large,jiang2024empowering}. For TSAD, recent works convert time series into textual or visual forms for LLM/MLLM-based anomaly detection, or explore time-text alignment for multimodal anomaly modeling \cite{alnegheimish2024large,xu2025can,hu2026towards}. However, these methods often rely on raw-value serialization, visualized inputs, external textual cues, or decision-level reasoning. In contrast, our method derives textual descriptions directly from the input time-series window and transforms them into predictive distributions aligned with numerical patch representations, allowing textual temporal information to participate in masked reconstruction and anomaly scoring.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Given an input window $\mathbf{X}\in \mathbb{R}^{L\times C}$, where $L$
denotes the window length and $C$ represents the number of variables, the
model learns a reconstruction mapping
$\widehat{\mathbf{X}}=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})$. During inference, the
reconstruction error serves as the anomaly score. Let $x_{t,c}$ and
$\hat{x}_{t,c}$ denote the $c$-th variable at timestamp $t$ of the input
and its reconstruction, respectively. The anomaly score is computed as
the channel-averaged reconstruction error:
\begin{equation}
    s_t =
    \frac{1}{C}
    \sum_{c=1}^{C}
    (x_{t,c}-\hat{x}_{t,c})^2 .
\end{equation}
A larger $s_t$ indicates that the observation is harder to reconstruct
under the learned normal patterns, and is therefore more likely to be
anomalous.

\subsection{Overview}

The proposed framework reconstructs each input window by jointly modeling numerical temporal patterns and structured textual descriptions derived from the time series itself. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}, \method{} consists of three main stages: (1) temporal patch encoding with masking, (2) dual-view textual modality construction, and (3) text-temporal alignment for masked reconstruction.

Given an input window $\mathbf{X}\in \mathbb{R}^{L\times C}$, we first apply window-level normalization:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\mathbf{X}} = \frac{\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{m}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}, \quad \boldsymbol{\sigma} = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{X})+\epsilon},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{m}$ and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ are computed along the temporal dimension and $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability. The normalized sequence is divided into patches with size $p$ and stride $r$, yielding $N_p = \lfloor(L-p)/r\rfloor + 1$ patches. Each patch is projected to dimension $d$ via a learnable linear projection $\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{patch}} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times d}$, followed by additive positional encoding. The resulting patch representation is $\mathbf{P}\in \mathbb{R}^{BC\times N_p\times d}$, where $B$ is the batch size and $d$ is the hidden dimension. A random patch mask is applied to construct incomplete temporal evidence. Both the complete and masked patch sequences are encoded by a shared Transformer encoder $E_t$, where $\mathrm{Mask}(\cdot)$ randomly zeros out a fraction of patch tokens:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{H}^{t}=E_t(\mathbf{P}), \quad \widetilde{\mathbf{H}}^{t} = E_t(\mathrm{Mask}(\mathbf{P})),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{H}^{t}$ serves as the complete temporal representation for alignment and $\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}^{t}$ serves as the masked temporal representation for reconstruction.

\subsection{Textual Modality Construction}

The textual modality is constructed from two complementary views of the
input window.

\textbf{Statistical prompt view.}
For each channel-wise patch, the model extracts statistical descriptors
including the minimum, maximum, and median values, and dominant lags
computed via autocorrelation. It also estimates a coarse window-level
trend direction from first-order differences across patches and inserts
this trend descriptor into each patch prompt. These descriptors are
formatted with dataset context into a natural-language prompt and encoded
by a frozen large language model. Let $\mathbf{H}^{\mathrm{llm}} \in
\mathbb{R}^{BCN_p \times M \times d_{\mathrm{llm}}}$ denote the
LLM hidden states, where $M$ is the maximum prompt length.
These are first projected to the temporal latent space via
$\mathbf{W}_{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{llm}} \times d}$,
flattened over the token dimension, and then projected to dimension $d$
via $\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{prompt}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(M \cdot d) \times d}$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{U}'
    &=
    \operatorname{flatten}\left(\Phi(\bar{\mathbf{X}})\mathbf{W}_{h}\right)
    \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{prompt}}, \\
    \mathbf{U}
    &=
    \operatorname{reshape}_{BC\times N_p\times d}(\mathbf{U}').
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi(\cdot)$ denotes the prompt construction function that
returns $\mathbf{H}^{\mathrm{llm}}$, and $\operatorname{flatten}(\cdot)$
reshapes $\mathbb{R}^{BCN_p \times M \times d}$ to
$\mathbb{R}^{BCN_p \times (M \cdot d)}$. Accordingly,
$\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{BC\times N_p\times d}$. Since the LLM
parameters are frozen, the language model serves as a text encoder
rather than a trainable anomaly detector.

\textbf{Structural component view.}
The raw input window is decomposed into trend, seasonal, and residual
components:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{X}
    =
    \mathbf{X}^{tr}
    +
    \mathbf{X}^{se}
    +
    \mathbf{X}^{re}.
\end{equation}
The trend component is obtained by a moving-average filter followed by
a linear temporal mapping; the seasonal component is estimated from the
detrended signal via a feed-forward network; and the residual component
is the remaining signal. Each component is independently normalized and
patch-embedded. Let $\mathbf{R}^{q} \in \mathbb{R}^{M\times d}$ denote the
LLM-encoded role prompt for component $q \in \{tr, se, re\}$. The
component patch embeddings are concatenated with their respective role
prompts and encoded by a component encoder $E_c$ shared across components:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{V}^{q}
    =
    \Pi_{\mathrm{patch}}
    \left(E_c\left([\mathbf{R}^{q}; \mathbf{P}^{q}]\right)\right),
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot;\cdot]$ denotes concatenation along the patch dimension,
and $\Pi_{\mathrm{patch}}(\cdot)$ selects the $N_p$ output tokens
corresponding to component patches after removing the role prompt tokens.
The encoded representations of the trend, seasonal, and residual
components are denoted as $\mathbf{V}^{tr}$,
$\mathbf{V}^{se}$, and $\mathbf{V}^{re}$, respectively. These are fused
via a learnable linear projection $\mathbf{W}_{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{3d \times d}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{V}
    =
    [\mathbf{V}^{tr};\mathbf{V}^{se};\mathbf{V}^{re}]
    \mathbf{W}_{c},
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot;\cdot;\cdot]$ denotes concatenation along the feature
dimension. Accordingly, $\mathbf{U}$ captures prompt-level statistical
features and $\mathbf{V}$ captures structure-aware temporal
features.

\subsection{Text-Temporal Alignment}

The two textual views are integrated via a cross-attention layer
followed by a feed-forward network. Denoting multi-head attention as
$\mathcal{A}(Q,K,V)$, layer normalization as $\mathrm{LN}(\cdot)$, and
a feed-forward network as $\mathrm{FFN}(\cdot)$, the fusion is
formulated as a standard Transformer block:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{Z}_1 &= \mathrm{LN}\left(\mathbf{U} + \mathcal{A}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{V})\right), \\
    \mathbf{Z} &= \mathrm{LN}\left(\mathbf{Z}_1 + \mathrm{FFN}(\mathbf{Z}_1)\right),
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{U}$ serves as the query and $\mathbf{V}$ serves as the
key and value. The output $\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{BC\times N_p\times d}$
is the fused textual representation of the input window.

To align the textual representation with the temporal representation,
we model the conditional distribution of the temporal patches given
the textual representation as a diagonal Gaussian distribution. The
textual representation predicts the mean and log-variance tensors:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu}=f_{\mu}(\mathbf{Z}), \quad
    \boldsymbol{\ell}=\operatorname{clip}(f_{\sigma}(\mathbf{Z})).
\end{equation}
where $f_{\mu}$ and $f_{\sigma}$ are linear projections and
$\boldsymbol{\ell}=\log\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}$. For each training
window, let $c\in\{1,\ldots,C\}$ index the channel dimension,
$i\in\{1,\ldots,N_p\}$ index the patch dimension, and
$j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}$ index the feature dimension. The conditional
distribution of each temporal patch is
\begin{equation}
    p(\mathbf{h}_{c,i}^{t} \mid \mathbf{z}_{c,i})
    =
    \mathcal{N}\left(
    \mathbf{h}_{c,i}^{t};
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c,i},
    \mathrm{diag}(\exp(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{c,i}))
    \right).
\end{equation}
The alignment objective minimizes the Gaussian negative log-likelihood
up to an additive constant:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}
    &=
    \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{D}}
    \Bigg[
    \frac{1}{2 C N_p d}
    \sum_{c=1}^{C}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N_p}
    \sum_{j=1}^{d}
    \Big(
    \ell_{c,i,j} \\
    &\quad+
    \big(\mathrm{sg}(h_{c,i,j}^{t})-\mu_{c,i,j}\big)^2
    \exp(-\ell_{c,i,j})
    \Big)
    \Bigg].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)$ denotes the stop-gradient operator that
prevents gradients from flowing into the temporal encoder. The operator
$\operatorname{clip}(\cdot)$ constrains each log-variance element to a
stable range. This probabilistic formulation
encourages the textual representation to predict both the location and
the uncertainty of the temporal representation, thereby accommodating the inherent
variability in text-temporal correspondences.

\subsection{Reconstruction Module}

The reconstruction module leverages the textual representation to guide
the recovery of masked temporal features. The masked temporal features
first undergo self-attention to aggregate partial temporal context:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathbf{H}}'^{t}
    =
    \mathcal{A}(
    \widetilde{\mathbf{H}}^{t}, \widetilde{\mathbf{H}}^{t}, \widetilde{\mathbf{H}}^{t}
    ).
\end{equation}
The textual representation then queries this self-attended temporal
memory via cross-attention, followed by a feed-forward network:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{G}_1 &= \mathrm{LN}\left(\mathbf{Z} + \mathcal{A}(\mathbf{Z}, \widetilde{\mathbf{H}}'^{t}, \widetilde{\mathbf{H}}'^{t})\right), \\
    \mathbf{G} &= \mathrm{LN}\left(\mathbf{G}_1 + \mathrm{FFN}(\mathbf{G}_1)\right),
\end{align}
where $\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}'^{t}$ serves as the key and value, and
$\mathbf{Z}$ serves as the query. This design enables reconstruction to
benefit from both textual regularities and partial temporal evidence.

Finally, $\mathbf{G}$ is flattened over patches and projected back to
the input space:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathbf{X}}
    =
    \operatorname{reshape}_{B\times L\times C}
    \left(\operatorname{flatten}(\mathbf{G})\mathbf{W}_{o}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_{o} \in \mathbb{R}^{(N_p \cdot d) \times L}$ is a
learnable output projection, and $\operatorname{flatten}(\cdot)$ reshapes
$\mathbf{G} \in \mathbb{R}^{BC\times N_p\times d}$ to
$\mathbb{R}^{BC\times (N_p \cdot d)}$. After inverse normalization, the
reconstruction is compared with the original input for anomaly scoring.

\subsection{Training Objective}

The reconstruction loss is the mean squared error between the input
and its reconstruction:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{D}}
    \left[
    \frac{1}{LC}
    \left\lVert
    \mathbf{X}-\widehat{\mathbf{X}}
    \right\rVert_F^2
    \right],
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}$ denotes the training-window distribution and
$\lVert\cdot\rVert_F$ denotes the Frobenius norm over the temporal and
channel dimensions.
The alignment loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}$ is the Gaussian
negative log-likelihood defined in the previous subsection.
The total loss combines reconstruction and alignment:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}
    =
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}
    +
    \lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the weighting coefficient for alignment loss.
By jointly optimizing these terms, the model learns normal temporal
reconstruction and probabilistic text-temporal alignment. During inference, timestamps
with larger reconstruction errors are assigned higher anomaly scores.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Datasets.}
We evaluate \method{} on six real-world time-series anomaly detection datasets spanning diverse domains: \textit{Weather} (meteorological measurements), \textit{Energy} (energy price and statistics), \textit{Environment} (air quality), and three financial-market series \textit{KR}, \textit{EWJ}, and \textit{MDT}. Weather, Energy, and Environment are collected from Time-MMD~\cite{liu2024timemmd}, while KR, EWJ, and MDT are derived from FNSPID~\cite{dong2024fnspid}. These datasets exhibit varied anomaly patterns, including abrupt local deviations, contextual changes, and sustained anomalous segments. Following the reconstruction-based anomaly detection protocol, all variables are standardized channel-wise, and dataset-specific sliding-window configurations are used rather than a single fixed window length.

\textbf{Baselines.}
We compare against a broad set of time-series backbones under the same sliding-window reconstruction protocol: linear models (Linear, DLinear, and NLinear~\cite{zeng2023dlinear}), patch- or convolution-based models (PatchTST~\cite{nie2023patchtst}, TimesNet~\cite{wu2023timesnet}, MICN~\cite{wang2023micn}, FiLM~\cite{zhou2022film}, and LightTS~\cite{zhang2022lightts}), attention-based models (Transformer, iTransformer~\cite{liu2024itransformer}, Crossformer~\cite{zhang2023crossformer}, Informer~\cite{zhou2021informer}, Reformer, and FEDformer~\cite{zhou2022fedformer}), and MindTS as the closest text-temporal multimodal baseline. All baselines are evaluated with the same reconstruction-based detection protocol for fair comparison.

\textbf{Evaluation Metrics.}
We adopt Affiliated-F1-score (Aff-F)~\cite{huet2022local} as the primary metric for segment-level detection quality and report it using the benchmark's mean-threshold protocol. We also report V-PR and V-ROC~\cite{paparrizos2022volume}, which evaluate range-aware detection quality over threshold and window variations.

\textbf{Implementation Details.}
\method{} is implemented in PyTorch. We use Qwen3-1.7B as the frozen LLM backbone and truncate it to six Transformer layers for efficiency; the final hidden states of the truncated LLM are projected as textual embeddings. The temporal encoder and the text-guided reconstruction branch each use one Transformer layer. Training uses Adam with an initial learning rate of $10^{-4}$ and the default step decay schedule for 5 epochs. Unless otherwise specified, the alignment loss is the text-to-time Gaussian NLL and its weight is set to $\lambda=1.0$. For the main Qwen3-1.7B experiments, we use dataset-specific window and patch settings: KR uses $L=24$, patch/stride $8/8$; EWJ uses $L=48$, patch/stride $6/6$; Environment uses $L=72$, patch/stride $6/6$; and MDT, Energy, and Weather use $L=24$, patch/stride $6/6$. The mask ratio is 0.3 for EWJ and 0.4 for the remaining datasets; the hidden dimensions $d/d_{ff}$ are 256/16, 256/512, 256/32, 64/64, 256/8, and 64/8 for KR, EWJ, MDT, Environment, Energy, and Weather, respectively.



\subsection{Main Results}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} shows that \method{} ranks first in all 18 dataset--metric settings formed by six datasets and three evaluation metrics. The advantage is particularly clear over MindTS, the most closely related multimodal baseline: the average Aff-F increases from 66.81\% to 70.67\%, a gain of 3.86 percentage points. The corresponding averages improve from 42.44\% to 49.54\% for V-PR and from 76.38\% to 82.25\% for V-ROC. Thus, the improvement is observed not only under the mean-threshold segment-level evaluation but also under threshold-independent, range-aware metrics, providing empirical support for distribution-based text-temporal integration.

The largest Aff-F margin occurs on EWJ, where \method{} exceeds the second-best result by 9.98 percentage points. Stable gains are also observed on Energy, KR, and MDT, with margins of 2.12, 2.28, and 4.53 percentage points, respectively. Together with the aggregate results, these representative cases indicate that the proposed design remains effective across datasets with different temporal characteristics, without attributing the gains to any single anomaly pattern.

\subsection{LLM Backbone Analysis}

Table~\ref{tab:llm_comparison} evaluates seven lightweight backbones with 1.0B--1.7B parameters. Their average Aff-F scores fall within a narrow range of approximately 70.07\%--70.67\%, spanning only 0.60 percentage points. This limited variation indicates that the framework is not highly sensitive to the choice of lightweight LLM backbone.

The best backbone nevertheless varies across datasets: SmolLM2-1.7B performs best on KR and MDT, whereas OLMo-1B attains the highest Aff-F on EWJ. TinyLlama-1.1B also reaches an average Aff-F of 70.43\%, remaining close to the best average despite its smaller size. These results support the compatibility of the framework with multiple lightweight LLMs. The observed stability is consistent with the combined roles of structured textual construction, probabilistic alignment, and text-guided reconstruction, rather than dependence on a particular backbone.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reveals a clear ordering in the contribution of the evaluated components. Excluding the entire text branch causes the largest reduction in average Aff-F, approximately 4.3 percentage points, while excluding text-guided reconstruction reduces it by approximately 3.0 points. By comparison, omitting the trend-, seasonal-, and residual-level component descriptions results in a smaller average decrease of approximately 1.4 points. The range-aware metrics exhibit a compatible pattern: the variants without the text branch or text-guided reconstruction each lose roughly nine percentage points in average V-PR, with substantial reductions also observed in V-ROC. These results indicate that the text modality supplies the main complementary information and that injecting it into masked reconstruction is an important part of realizing this benefit; component-level semantics provide an additional, but more moderate, structural contribution. Accordingly, numerical reconstruction alone is less effective on these benchmarks.

The role of the alignment objective is further examined by setting $\lambda=0$ in the sensitivity study.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity Analysis}

Figure~\ref{fig:hyperparameter} shows dataset-dependent responses to the mask ratio. On Environment, Aff-F reaches a relatively high level around ratios of 0.4--0.5. Energy remains comparatively stable from 0.1 to 0.5 but declines markedly at 0.6. The results indicate that moderate masking provides a robust operating range, whereas excessive masking can impair reconstruction quality, as is particularly evident on Energy. At lower ratios, the reconstruction task may become less challenging because more temporal context remains visible, although the figure does not imply a uniform degradation across datasets.

For the alignment-loss weight, Aff-F on both Environment and Energy improves overall as $\lambda$ increases from 0 to 1, while $\lambda=2$ yields no further improvement. This behavior provides evidence that the Gaussian alignment objective is beneficial, while also indicating that its contribution should remain balanced with the reconstruction objective rather than dominate optimization.






\subsection{Visualization and Case Study}

Figure~\ref{fig:gaussian_nll} examines the internal text-to-time alignment behavior. The temporal NLL curve contains pronounced local fluctuations, so it should not be interpreted as a pointwise anomaly detector. More reliable evidence is provided by the distributional comparison: anomalous patches exhibit a higher NLL median and a wider distribution than normal patches, indicating a stronger representation mismatch between these patches and the Gaussian distribution predicted from text. This diagnostic is consistent with the intended role of probabilistic alignment. Alignment NLL is used only to inspect this internal behavior; the final anomaly score is still derived from reconstruction error.

Figure~\ref{fig:anomaly_score_grid} presents the resulting reconstruction-based detection behavior on six datasets with different fluctuation patterns. The scores generally exhibit elevated responses around anomalous intervals, qualitatively complementing the aggregate results in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}. Local peaks also occur outside some annotated intervals, so these examples are best viewed as evidence of broadly consistent responses rather than exact localization of every anomaly.
